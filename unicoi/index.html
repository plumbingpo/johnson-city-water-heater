Getting More Years From Your Water Heater in Unicoi

The best way to avoid a surprise leak is simple maintenance. We recommend that homeowners flush the tank once a year (more often with hard water), have the anode rod checked every three to five years, lift the T&P valve lever once a year to make sure it isn't stuck, and keep the thermostat around 120 degrees, which limits scalding risk and cuts down on scale. Tankless owners should plan on descaling once a year if their water is hard.

Age matters too. Most tank water heaters run 8 to 12 years, tankless units often reach 20 with regular descaling, and heat pump models usually last 10 to 15. If your heater is past that window and showing warning signs like discolored hot water, popping and rumbling, moisture around the base, or showers that go cold sooner, a planned replacement is almost always cheaper and much easier on you than an emergency one after the tank lets go.

Minor issues don't stay minor for long. Consider a spent anode rod: replacing it is a short, affordable repair, but once it's gone, the water starts eating the tank itself, and just like that a minor fix becomes a full replacement. That's why we encourage homeowners to keep our number in their phone and call at the first sign of trouble. Catching it early saves time and money and prevents a flooded utility room.

What Goes Wrong With Local Water Heaters

The majority of water heater failures we see in Unicoi start with the water itself. Depending on whether a home is on municipal water or a private well, hard water may be part of the picture: dissolved minerals settle to the bottom of a tank as sediment, forcing the burner or lower element to work harder, which produces the popping and rumbling many homeowners hear when the heater fires. In tankless units, the same minerals build scale inside the heat exchanger, which is why annual descaling matters so much where the water is hard.

Equipment age plays a big role too. Once the sacrificial anode rod wears out, usually somewhere in the second half of a tank's life, the steel begins corroding from within, and a drip from the bottom seam is usually the first visible sign. The area has a mix of fuel types, gas tanks in some houses and electric tanks in others, which means we service both sides: thermocouples, burners, and venting on gas units, and elements, thermostats, and breakers on electric ones.

Code requirements add another layer. Swapping out an older heater often means bringing the install up to current code: an expansion tank if the water system is closed, a T&P discharge line piped to an approved spot, venting that drafts properly on gas units, and a drain pan or seismic straps where required. Well water brings its own challenges, such as heavier sediment, iron staining, and the rotten-egg smell that sometimes develops when the water reacts with a standard anode rod. Every one of these problems is fixable, and most are much cheaper to address early. If something seems off, a quick diagnostic visit catches the small problem before it becomes an expensive one.
